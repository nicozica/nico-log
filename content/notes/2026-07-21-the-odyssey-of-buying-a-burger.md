Today I went in to buy a burger and ended up going through a kind of digital obstacle course that seemed designed to find out how much effort I was willing to put in before giving up and going somewhere else to eat.

First I went to the cashier, like I always did, but they told me I could only pay in cash or with MODO. I never use MODO, even though I had the app installed, so I opened it to see if I could handle it there. Before showing me any useful option it asked me to update it.

I did not really feel like updating the app at the register just to buy a burger, so I decided to try one of the self-service kiosks. In theory, those machines are there to speed up the purchase, avoid lines, and let you place the order at your own pace. In practice, the first thing it did was ask me to log in.

I just wanted to buy a burger, not create an account or start a long-term relationship with the company. It took me a while to find the option to skip that step, and it was much less visible than the buttons inviting me to register or sign in.

Once inside, the menu did not help much either. The screen was full of giant photos, promotions, combos, and featured products, while the descriptive text was minimal. There was a lot of visual information, but not much useful information to understand what each product included or what the concrete difference was between one option and another.

Choosing the burger itself was not especially hard. What was hard was ordering the regular version, because the whole interface, spread across a vertical 32-inch screen less than half a meter away from me (a format that already goes against our panoramic vision), seemed designed to push me toward the bigger and more expensive option. The larger sizes, add-ons, and upgrades had huge photos and flashy buttons. The normal version was there, but you had to find it among all the opportunities to spend a little more.

After rejecting a bunch of extras, I finally completed the order and got to payment. The kiosk showed two options and I chose Mercado Pago, assuming it would give me a QR code to scan with my phone, like almost any other place.

I stood there waiting in front of the screen without really understanding what I was supposed to do. After a few seconds an employee came over and explained that the process worked the other way around: I did not have to scan a code generated by the kiosk, but open Mercado Pago, generate my own payment QR, and point it at the machine's reader.

That option was not particularly obvious inside the app either. I ended up entering a section I mainly recognized because I had seen it used to pay for public transit, not for buying food. After finding the code, I pointed it at the reader and waited for it to recognize it.

It did not read it.

We tried again, moving the phone, changing the distance, and bringing it a little closer to the sensor. The reader still would not react until, all at once, the kiosk canceled the whole operation and went back to the initial screen. The entire order disappeared.

The employee ended up taking me to the kiosk next to it and typing the burger in from scratch himself. The self-service system had needed human help to complete a purchase that the same person could originally have handled at a cashier in less than a minute.

The funny thing is that each individual decision in the process probably makes sense on its own. The company's app tries to build loyalty, the kiosk reduces the number of cashiers, the big photos help sell products, and the featured options increase the average order value. The problem appears when all those decisions stack up and nobody seems to evaluate the whole experience from the point of view of the person who just wants to eat something.

In a few minutes I had to open an app I never use, discover that it needed an update, find a way around the login, navigate an interface designed to promote the most expensive options, interpret an unusual payment mechanism, and repeat the whole order because a reader could not recognize a code.

Self-service should remove steps and make an everyday task faster. When it is poorly designed, it just shifts the company's work onto the customer and turns a simple operation into a patience test.

I had gone in to buy a burger. The badly used technology did everything it could to stop me.
